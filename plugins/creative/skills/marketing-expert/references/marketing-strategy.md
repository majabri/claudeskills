# Marketing Strategy

Marketing strategy is the set of decisions about *who we are for, what category we're in, and what we want them to believe about us* that everything else (brand, copy, design, sales, ads) is in service of.

If strategy is wrong, downstream work is impossible to do well — you'll write better and better headlines for the wrong audience, design more polished pages around the wrong promise, and run more campaigns that don't compound. If strategy is right, downstream work tends to *land* almost regardless of polish, because the underlying claim is true and well-targeted.

Most "marketing isn't working" problems are strategy problems. Treat them as such before reaching for tactical fixes.

## The core questions strategy answers

Before any campaign, headline, or page, you should be able to answer:

1. **Who are the best-fit customers?** Specific people in specific situations who get the most value from this product, not "everyone."
2. **What are they choosing between?** What's their actual alternative today (often "do nothing" or a spreadsheet, not the obvious competitor).
3. **What's distinctive about this product** that genuinely matters to those people? Capabilities competitors lack — and the *value* of those capabilities.
4. **What category** are we in (in the customer's mind)? Categories are the cognitive shelf customers reach for; getting the category wrong costs you the deal before pitch.
5. **What's the one thing** we want this audience to think about us when they hear our name?

If any of these are fuzzy, fix that first. Polish on top of fuzz is wasted work.

## April Dunford's positioning framework

The most usable, battle-tested positioning methodology for B2B and product-led companies. Five components, worked in sequence.

### 1. Competitive alternatives

What would your customer use if your product didn't exist? Not "what would they use instead of us" — what would they actually use. Often this is:

- A direct competitor
- A DIY solution (spreadsheet, a hacky internal script, a manual process)
- A different category of product (a CRM as a project tracker, email as a CRM)
- Doing nothing

The point isn't to enumerate competitors — it's to understand the *frame of reference* in your customer's head. Customers don't compare against an objective ideal; they compare against what they'd otherwise do.

Dunford's classic example: Bugatti's competitor isn't Ferrari — it's yachts, because at that price point the customer is shopping for status objects. If you don't know what your real alternatives are, you don't know what game you're playing.

### 2. Differentiated capabilities

What can you do that your alternatives can't? Be honest and specific. Not "we're easier to use" (every competitor claims that) — what *specifically* do you do that they specifically can't?

If you can't name 1–3 differentiated capabilities, you have a serious problem: either the product isn't differentiated, or you don't yet know what's differentiated about it. Both are addressable, but you have to face them.

### 3. Value (so what?) of those capabilities

Each capability needs a "so what?" — what's the customer outcome it produces? Run the *so what?* test ruthlessly:

- Capability: "We integrate with 50+ CRMs."
- So what? "Your team doesn't have to switch tools."
- So what? "Less context-switching, faster sales cycles."
- So what? "Reps close 15% more deals per quarter."

Stop when you reach a customer-relevant outcome. The earlier "so whats" are features; the later ones are values. Customers buy values, not features.

### 4. Best-fit customers

Who values your specific differentiated value the most? This is rarely "everyone in the category." It's a subsegment with a specific profile.

If you don't know the profile, study your *current best customers* — the ones who renew, expand, and refer. What's true of them that isn't true of customers who churn? Often the answer is much narrower than "B2B SaaS companies": "B2B SaaS companies between Series B and Series D, with 50–200 reps, where the head of sales is a former individual contributor and the CRM was implemented in the last 18 months."

Best-fit customer focus is scary because it feels like you're "leaving money on the table." In practice, narrower targeting almost always grows revenue faster than broad targeting, because the messaging is sharper, the sales motion is repeatable, and the customers refer each other.

### 5. Market category

What category are we in, in the customer's mind? Categories matter because they trigger expectations: pricing norms, sales cycles, who attends meetings, what features are table stakes, what budget line item the spend comes from.

You have three positioning moves on category:

- **Win an existing category** — be the obvious leader of a defined category. Easiest if you can credibly claim it; nearly impossible if a 10x larger incumbent owns it.
- **Reframe within a category** — accept the category but redefine what "winning" means in it. ("Most CRMs are built for managers; ours is built for reps.")
- **Create a new category** — invent the language that categorizes you. Highest risk and highest reward (think: "marketing automation" before HubSpot, "enterprise data warehouse" before Snowflake's framing). Requires teaching the market a new word, which is expensive — only do it if you can lead it.

The most common error is *implicitly* picking a category by default — your product has a name and a feature list, customers slot you somewhere, and that somewhere is wrong. Decide the category on purpose.

## Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

A complementary framework, especially useful for understanding *why* customers buy.

The core idea: customers don't buy products; they "hire" products to do a job. The job is the underlying progress they're trying to make. The product is one of several candidates for that hire.

Practical use:

- **Identify the job:** What progress is the customer trying to make? Often functional (close the books faster), but always with emotional and social dimensions ("I want to look competent to my CFO").
- **Identify the alternatives** they could hire for the job: this loops back to Dunford's competitive alternatives.
- **Find the constraint** that's keeping the alternatives from doing the job well: this is your opportunity.

Clayton Christensen's classic example: people don't buy a milkshake because they're hungry; they "hire" a milkshake to make their morning commute interesting. Once you know that, you compete with bagels and coffee, not with other milkshakes.

JTBD interviews — talking to recent customers about *the moment they switched* and what was happening in their lives — produce better strategy inputs than feature surveys. Whenever you have access to real customers, do this kind of interview.

## Messaging hierarchy

Once positioning is decided, messaging cascades from it. A working hierarchy:

1. **Positioning statement** (internal, not customer-facing) — your single source of truth for who you are and why you matter, against alternatives, for whom.
2. **Value proposition** (1 sentence) — the customer-facing version. What you do, for whom, why it matters, and what's different.
3. **Pillars** (3–5) — the supporting claims that prove the value prop. Each pillar maps to a real differentiated capability.
4. **Proof points** under each pillar — concrete evidence (features, data, customer outcomes, testimonials).

Every piece of marketing copy, every page, every pitch should be traceable to this hierarchy. If a page is making claims not in the hierarchy, either the page is off-strategy or the hierarchy is incomplete (often the latter — find the missing pillar and add it formally).

### Geoffrey Moore's positioning template

Useful for forcing yourself to fill in the blanks:

> For [target customer]
> who [statement of need or opportunity],
> our [product/service name] is a [product category]
> that [statement of key benefit — that is, compelling reason to buy].
> Unlike [primary competitive alternative],
> our product [statement of primary differentiation].

This is a starting structure, not a final tagline. Treat the output as a positioning *artifact*, not consumer-facing copy. Use `copywriting` to turn the underlying positioning into actual language that lands.

## Audience: who, really?

Most marketers treat "audience" too vaguely. Sharper segmentation:

### Best-fit profile

Specific, observable characteristics of customers who get the most value:

- Industry / vertical
- Size (revenue, headcount, scale of relevant problem)
- Stage / maturity
- Tech stack or operating context
- Trigger event ("recently raised a Series B," "just hired a head of finance")
- The specific *role* of the buyer and *role* of the user (often different people)

Resist "VPs of Marketing at companies between $5M and $500M ARR." That's the entire universe of potential buyers — not a segment. Segment by something *behavioral* or *situational*, not just by demographic.

### Buyer personas (use sparingly)

Personas have a bad reputation because they're often generated as fictional avatars with names and stock photos, then never used. Useful personas are short and behavioral:

> *The skeptical practitioner.* Senior individual contributor, has been burned by tools that promised efficiency and delivered overhead. Reads HN, dismisses marketing language. Buys when peers vouch for the product, not from ads. Will champion internally if convinced — won't champion if convinced "the marketing department wrote this."

That's a persona that changes how you write. "Sarah, age 34, enjoys yoga" is not.

### B2B buying committees

In B2B, the "audience" is rarely one person — it's a buying committee with different roles:

- **Champion** — the user/practitioner who'll fight for you internally
- **Buyer/decision-maker** — the budget owner who signs off
- **Influencers** — peers, advisors, technical reviewers
- **Blockers** — security, legal, procurement (usually risk-averse, not pro-product)

Each role has different concerns and reads different content. The champion needs ammunition to sell internally. The buyer needs ROI/business case. Blockers need risk-mitigation (security pages, compliance docs, contract simplicity). Plan messaging by role, not just by "audience."

## Differentiation

Most companies are not as differentiated as they think. The honest test:

> Take your homepage. Replace your logo and product name with a competitor's. Show it to someone unfamiliar with both companies. Can they tell which company it's about?

If the answer is "no" or "not really," you don't have differentiation in your messaging — you have category-default messaging.

The fix isn't louder claims of differentiation — it's *finding actually-differentiated capabilities* (Dunford step 2) and *describing them concretely* (`copywriting`). Vague claims of being "different" don't differentiate; specific facts do.

### Sources of real differentiation

- **Capability** — you can do something competitors can't.
- **Audience focus** — you're built specifically for a segment competitors treat generically.
- **Operating model** — you sell, deliver, or support the product fundamentally differently (open-source vs. closed, self-serve vs. enterprise sales, fixed-fee vs. usage-based).
- **Stance / point of view** — you take a public position the category doesn't, and that position attracts the customers who agree.
- **Speed / scale / depth** on a dimension that matters.

If you can't find any of these honestly, the strategic conversation is about *the product*, not about marketing. Marketing can't manufacture differentiation that doesn't exist; it can only express differentiation that does.

## Go-to-market and launch

A launch plan that works has the following decisions made *before* execution starts:

1. **What we're launching, in one sentence** — the news, in customer language.
2. **Who the launch is for** — primary audience, with channels they actually use.
3. **What we want them to do** — one specific call to action, at one specific surface.
4. **What proof we'll lead with** — customers, data, demo, partnership.
5. **The story arc** — pre-launch teaser (if any), launch day, post-launch sustain.
6. **The owned surfaces** — landing page, email, in-product banner, social posts.
7. **The earned moves** — press, partnerships, communities, podcasts.
8. **The paid amplification** (if any) — channels, budget, audience targets.
9. **Success metrics** — what we'll look at on day 1, week 1, and quarter 1.

Common launch failures:

- **Launching the feature, not the change to the customer.** "Now with X capability" is a feature. "Save 10 hours a week with X" is a change. Lead with the change.
- **One spike, no sustain.** Launches that get one big day of attention and then disappear from the brand's surfaces are worth a fraction of launches that keep paying off for weeks via case studies, webinars, deeper dives.
- **No before/after measurement.** Without baseline metrics, you can't tell if the launch worked.

## Campaigns

Campaigns are time-bounded marketing efforts oriented around a single message, audience, and outcome. The discipline is:

- **One specific message** (not a smorgasbord of value props)
- **One specific audience** (a subsegment, not the whole TAM)
- **One specific outcome** (a defined behavior change with a measurable result)
- **Multiple coordinated surfaces** (the same message expressed appropriately on web, email, ads, social, sales — not five different messages on five surfaces)

Campaigns fail when they try to say multiple things to multiple audiences in multiple channels with no central spine. Pick the spine; tune the surfaces.

## Investor / fundraising messaging vs. customer messaging

These are distinct disciplines often conflated:

- **Customer messaging** answers "should I buy this?" — focus on *job-to-be-done*, differentiated value, proof.
- **Investor messaging** answers "could this be a $1B+ company?" — focus on *market size*, defensibility, growth trajectory, team, why-now.

A pitch deck for investors and a homepage for customers should not be the same artifact. Customers want to know what changes for them; investors want to know how big this can get and why this team will be the one to do it. Use the right register for the right audience.

## Common strategy failure modes

- **"We're for everyone."** Almost always a sign that the team hasn't done the work to identify their best-fit segment. Narrow.
- **Feature-led messaging.** Talking about *what we built* instead of *what changes for the customer*. Run the so-what test.
- **Following the category leader.** Mirroring the leader's positioning makes you look like a knockoff. Find a different angle (audience focus, stance, model).
- **Strategy drift.** Sales says one thing, marketing says another, the website says a third, the founder pitches a fourth. Customers feel the incoherence even if they can't name it. Align via a single positioning artifact everyone references.
- **Strategy by metaphor.** "We're the Uber for X" can be a useful internal shorthand and is almost always a bad public positioning — it cedes the category to someone else and makes you sound derivative.
- **Skipping the customer.** Doing strategy in conference rooms without talking to recent customers is the most common and most expensive failure. Always pressure-test against real customer voice.

## Working with the user

When the user says marketing isn't working, get to the strategy layer fast. The diagnostic questions, in order:

1. "Who are your *best* current customers — the ones who renew and refer? What do they have in common?"
2. "What were they using *before* you, and what did they switch from?"
3. "Why did they switch — in their words?"
4. "What do they tell *other* people you do, when they refer you?"
5. "Is the way you describe yourselves on the homepage the same as how those customers describe you?"

In most strategy gaps, those five questions reveal the gap quickly: the marketing is describing a generic product to a generic audience, while the actual customers have a specific story about a specific change for a specific situation. The fix is to align the marketing to the customers' real story.

## When to hand off to other skills

- The strategy is clear; now it needs to be expressed in *language* (headlines, body, taglines, ads) → `copywriting`.
- The strategy is clear; now it needs to be expressed in *visual identity* (look, feel, name, logo) → `brand-identity`.
- The strategy is clear; now it needs to be expressed on *digital surfaces* (website, landing pages, product UI) → `digital-design`.
- Strategy involves claims about competitors, comparison advertising, or use of others' marks/content → `creative-rights` (especially for comparative claims and use of competitors' trademarks in marketing).
