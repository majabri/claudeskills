# Copywriting

Copywriting is writing where the *function* of the words matters more than their flourish. Every line earns its place by doing a job: clarifying, persuading, instructing, or differentiating. Decoration without function is fat — cut it.

The job is *not* "write something nice." The job is to find the shortest, most specific, most truthful expression of an idea that will land with a particular reader in a particular moment.

## The non-negotiables

These are the failure modes that make most copy bad. Internalize them and you'll catch most issues before drafting.

### 1. Specific beats abstract

Almost every weak line of copy is too abstract. "Powerful platform for modern teams" is meaningless because every adjective applies to every competitor. "Helps Series B SaaS founders cut their finance team's monthly close from 11 days to 2" is specific, testable, and impossible to confuse with another product.

Test every line: would the *opposite* statement be obviously wrong? "Slow platform for outdated teams" — nobody claims that, so its opposite ("powerful platform for modern teams") tells the reader nothing. Specific claims have testable opposites and survive scrutiny.

When you find yourself reaching for "modern," "powerful," "innovative," "intuitive," "seamless," "robust," "world-class," "next-generation," "cutting-edge," "transform," or "empower" — stop. Those are placeholder words for "I don't know what specific true thing to say." Find the specific thing.

### 2. Verbs do the work; adjectives often don't

Strong verbs are the spine of strong copy. Compare:

- Adjective-heavy: "A powerful, intuitive, comprehensive financial platform that enables seamless collaboration."
- Verb-heavy: "Close the books in two days. Catch errors before your CFO does. Hand auditors a clean trail."

The second tells you what the product *does to the world*. The first tells you what the marketing department feels about itself.

When editing, scan for adjectives. For each one, ask: does this adjective do work the noun and verb couldn't already do? If not, kill it. Most adjectives are scaffolding the writer leaves in the building.

### 3. Audience first, every time

Who exactly is this for? What do they currently know? What do they not yet know? What do they care about? What language do they themselves use?

Copy that imagines a generic "user" or "customer" sounds generic. Copy that imagines a *specific person on a specific Tuesday* — the founder reviewing this on her phone between meetings, the engineer on call at 2am, the busy parent at the school pickup line — sounds like it's actually for someone.

Match the audience's vocabulary, not your own. If they call it "the close," don't call it "the financial period reconciliation cycle." If they say "billing," don't say "monetization."

### 4. Voice over volume

Most copy is too long. Long copy isn't necessarily bad — long-form sales pages, brand stories, narrative content all have their place. But length should be earned, not defaulted to. Each sentence should pay its way: it's making a new point, deepening a current point, or providing a needed transition.

Cut everything else. The standard editing pass on most drafts is a 30–50% cut, and the result is sharper.

### 5. Distinctive voice beats neutral correctness

A correctly-written but voiceless paragraph is worse than a flawed one with personality, *if* the flaws are minor. Brand voice is an asset; bland competence is forgettable. The strongest brands have voices that sound like a specific kind of human:

- Mailchimp: helpful friend, dry humor, plain language
- Stripe: precise engineer, respects intelligence, no fluff
- Liquid Death: punk rock, relentless joke commitment, brand as bit
- Notion: optimistic generalist, slightly philosophical

Your brand voice should be similarly defensible — you should be able to describe it in a sentence and recognize it across surfaces.

## Voice and tone

**Voice** is the brand's stable personality (steady across surfaces and moments). **Tone** modulates within that voice for context (a billing error message is more careful than a celebratory onboarding screen).

### Defining a brand voice

A working voice definition is short and specific. The pattern that holds up:

> We sound like [archetype / kind of person], because [why this fits the brand and audience]. Our voice is [3 adjectives] but never [3 things we're often mistaken for].

For example:
> We sound like a senior engineer who's seen the failure modes and learned to communicate clearly, because our customers are technical and time-poor. We're precise, dry, and helpful — but never academic, jargon-heavy, or self-congratulatory.

Then: **show, don't tell**. The most usable voice guides include 6–10 do/don't pairs:

> ❌ "Leverage our innovative platform to transform your workflow."
> ✅ "Save your team three hours a week. Here's how it works."

> ❌ "We're thrilled to announce some exciting updates!"
> ✅ "Three things changed in this release. Here's what."

Without paired examples, voice docs read as theory and don't change behavior.

### Tone modulation

Within a stable voice, tone shifts by context:

| Moment | Voice (constant) | Tone (variable) |
|---|---|---|
| Onboarding | Helpful expert | Warm, encouraging |
| Routine UI label | Helpful expert | Quiet, neutral |
| Error message | Helpful expert | Calm, owns the problem, helpful |
| Marketing campaign | Helpful expert | Confident, slightly bold |
| Outage / incident | Helpful expert | Direct, accountable, sober |

When in doubt, err toward calmer than louder. It's easier to gain energy than to recover from being shrill.

## Headlines

The headline is doing more work than any other line of copy. It either earns the rest of the read or loses the audience. The best headline:

- Is **specific** (one promise, not a vague aura)
- Is **for a person** (you can imagine who reads this and nods)
- Is **about them**, not about you (their problem, their gain, their world — not your platform's features)
- Is **short** enough to read in one glance
- Has a **claim** strong enough to want to verify

### Headline patterns that work

These are not formulas to follow blindly — they're patterns to know so you can choose the right one for the situation, or break them on purpose.

- **The specific promise:** "Cut your monthly close from 11 days to 2."
- **The category-defining claim:** "The first ___ for ___."
- **The contrarian truth:** "Most CRMs make sales worse. Here's the one built for sellers."
- **The named-audience hook:** "For founders who hate finance."
- **The question that lands the problem:** "Why does it take three weeks to close a six-figure deal?"
- **The before/after compression:** "From spreadsheet chaos to one source of truth — in a week."
- **The unexpected metaphor:** "Your data warehouse, but for humans."
- **The radical simplicity:** "Email, but good." (only works if you can defend it)

### Headline anti-patterns

- **The brand-self-portrait:** "We are a leading global provider of cloud-based solutions." Nobody cares. Make the headline about *them*, not you.
- **The vague-future:** "The future of [category]." Lazy and meaningless. What specifically about the future, and why should the reader believe you?
- **The triple-stack:** "Powerful. Intuitive. Built for teams." Three adjectives in a row is a confession of having nothing specific to say.
- **The corporate empower:** "Empowering organizations to transform." This sentence has been written 10,000 times and means nothing.

### Headline process

1. Write the **brief in one sentence:** what audience, what action, what specific true claim. (If you can't write this, do `marketing-strategy` work first.)
2. Generate **15–25 candidates** across multiple patterns. Don't self-edit while generating.
3. Cut to top 5 by gut. Read them aloud — does it sound like a human said this?
4. Pressure-test: would a competitor write this? If yes, it's not differentiated.
5. Test on a real reader if possible. "What does this make you think this product is?" tells you everything.

## Subheads, body, and supporting copy

The subhead's job is to *deepen* the headline, not restate it. If the headline is the claim, the subhead is the proof or the elaboration:

> **Headline:** Close the books in two days, not eleven.
> **Subhead:** Automated reconciliation, audit-ready exports, and a finance dashboard your CFO will actually look at.

Body copy should follow the *inverted pyramid* — most important first, supporting evidence after, details last. People rarely read to the end; ensure the top carries the weight.

### Concrete > abstract, every time

Replace abstract claims with concrete demonstrations:

- ❌ "Designed for scale."
- ✅ "Tested up to 50,000 concurrent users with sub-100ms response times."

- ❌ "World-class customer support."
- ✅ "Average response time: 8 minutes. Real engineers, not bots."

- ❌ "Trusted by leading companies."
- ✅ "Used by Stripe, Shopify, and 3,200 other companies."

If you can't concretize a claim, the claim probably isn't true and certainly isn't useful.

## CTAs and microcopy

### CTAs

CTA copy is one of the highest-leverage lines on any surface. Get it specific and active:

- ❌ "Submit," "Get started," "Click here," "Learn more"
- ✅ "Start your 14-day trial," "Book a 20-minute demo," "See sample data," "Read the pricing"

The user should know what happens *next* if they click. Reduce uncertainty.

### Form labels and helper text

- Use sentence case ("Email address"), not all caps ("EMAIL ADDRESS").
- Place labels above inputs (faster scanning, better mobile).
- If the field needs explanation, write helper text — don't cram it into the label.
- "Required" should be implicit (mark optional fields, not required ones, when most are required).

### Error messages

Error messages reveal the brand's character more than any marketing copy. The pattern that always works:

1. **State what happened**, in the user's terms (not the system's).
2. **Take responsibility** where appropriate ("We couldn't load your data" — not "Error 503: backend unavailable").
3. **Tell them what to do** (retry, edit something, contact support).

Bad: "Error 422: Validation failed."
Better: "Your password needs at least 12 characters and one number. Try again?"

### Empty states

Empty states are content opportunities, not blank space. Tell the user:

- Why this is empty (haven't created anything yet, no matches for filters)
- What to do (create the first one, change filters, learn how)
- Optionally: a sample, a video, a starter template

### Loading states

If something will take more than ~1 second, tell the user what's happening. "Loading…" is acceptable but generic. "Checking 3,400 transactions…" is alive. Don't lie about progress (fake progress bars destroy trust on the second use).

## Long-form content (blog, articles, newsletters)

When the user wants substantive written content — a blog post, article, newsletter — the same principles apply at a larger scale.

### Structure that respects the reader's time

A long piece should pay off the headline within the first 3 paragraphs. Don't bury the main point in a windup. Lead with the strongest version of the argument, then provide the depth that earns the headline.

A useful structure for most thought-leadership writing:

1. **Hook** — a specific anecdote, statistic, or question that signals you have something specific to say.
2. **Thesis** — the one-sentence point of the whole piece.
3. **Why this matters** — why the reader should care, in concrete terms.
4. **Argument / story / breakdown** — the substance, organized in a way that's easy to follow.
5. **What to do with this** — make it actionable; vague essays are forgettable.
6. **Close** — a single durable line that's quotable.

### The first paragraph rule

If the first paragraph could open a hundred different articles, it's not pulling its weight. Make it specific to this piece — a particular moment, observation, number, or question that signals "you're going to read something distinct."

### Length is earned

Don't pad to hit a word count. A 600-word piece that says one true thing well beats a 2,000-word piece that says it diluted by filler. SEO-driven length targets corrode quality; if you must hit a length, hit it with depth, not repetition.

## Writing for AI / LLM era

Two practical shifts:

- **Distinct voice has more value, not less.** Generic competent writing is now a commodity LLMs produce by the page. Specific, voiced, opinionated writing is where humans (and well-prompted humans-with-AI) still win.
- **AI-assisted drafting is fine; AI-shipped output usually isn't.** Use AI to break blank-page paralysis, generate variations, pressure-test headlines, find the right verb. Don't ship un-edited AI prose — it has tells (over-balanced sentences, "it's important to note that," excessive hedging, sentence-rhythm sameness, and abstract phrasing).

When editing AI-generated copy, look especially for:

- "It's important to note that…" / "It's worth mentioning that…" → cut
- Lists where every bullet is the same length and rhythm → vary
- "Whether you're a small business or a Fortune 500 company…" → pick a real audience
- Concluding paragraphs that just restate the intro → cut, end on a strong line

## Review checklist

When critiquing copy (yours or someone else's), in order:

1. **Audience clarity** — Could you state in one sentence who this is for and what state they're in?
2. **One claim** — Is there one specific, true, defensible claim, or is the copy stacked with vague benefits?
3. **Specific over abstract** — Are claims concrete and verifiable, or vague and decorative?
4. **Verbs and nouns over adjectives** — Is the work being done by strong verbs, or covered with adjectives?
5. **Voice consistency** — Does this sound like the same brand as the rest of the copy?
6. **Tone fit** — Is the tone right for this moment (onboarding vs. error vs. ad)?
7. **Length earned** — Does every sentence pay its way?
8. **Read aloud** — Does it sound like a person? Does it stumble anywhere?
9. **CTA clarity** — Does the reader know what to do next, with what consequence?

## When to hand off to other skills

- The copy is structurally fine but the *positioning is wrong* (we're saying the wrong thing to the wrong audience) → `marketing-strategy`.
- The page or screen *containing* the copy needs design work → `digital-design`.
- The brand voice isn't yet defined and copy keeps drifting → `brand-identity` (which covers voice as part of identity), in tandem with this skill for the linguistic work.
- Concerns about using copyrighted text, song lyrics, quoted material, or AI-generated content commercially → `creative-rights`.
