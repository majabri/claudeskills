# What Separates Traders Who Last (evidence + practice)

## 1. Base rates: know the odds you're playing against
- Taiwan, every day trade 1992–2006 (Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean): only a small
  minority (~1%) were predictably profitable net of fees. Aggregate day-trader
  performance was negative in almost every year. Survival was low: fewer than
  half still trading after a year.
- Brazil, 2013–2015 index-futures day traders (Chague, De-Losso, Giovannetti 2020):
  97% of those who persisted 300+ days lost money, with no evidence of learning by doing.
- Barber & Odean: the most active individual investors earn the least. Turnover and
  costs eat returns.
Implication: frequency is not an edge. The PDT repeal (June 2026) removes a barrier,
not a risk. Fewer, higher-quality swing trades beat many intraday ones for a
part-time operator.

## 2. What the research says works (and its limits)
- **Momentum / trend persistence** is among the most documented market anomalies
  (time-series and cross-sectional momentum; a century of trend-following evidence,
  Hurst/Ooi/Pedersen). Swing trading with the trend and relative strength
  leans on this. Momentum can crash sharply at market turning points (e.g. after
  bear-market bottoms), so regime checks matter.
- **Stop-losses** (Kaminski & Lo): under a pure random walk, stops lower expected
  return. When returns show momentum or regime switching, stop rules can add value
  and cut volatility. So stops belong in trend/momentum strategies. For
  mean-reversion trades (buying dips expecting a bounce), tight stops fight the
  strategy; size smaller and use wider, structure-based invalidation.
- **Tail hedging**: systematic OTM index puts have negative long-run returns (implied
  volatility usually exceeds realized). Trend following has historically been cheaper
  protection in prolonged bear markets but less reliable in sudden crashes. Layer
  both rather than rely on one.
- **Volatility-aware sizing** (sizing by ATR / risk-parity style) keeps any one
  volatile name from dominating P&L. Practitioner-standard, consistent with the
  literature on volatility-managed portfolios.
- **Costs and taxes** decide net results: spreads, option slippage, short-term gain
  tax rates, margin interest. A gross edge of +0.1R can round to zero after costs.

## 3. Habits of the durable minority (practitioner consensus)
1. **Written plan before entry**: trigger, stop, target, size, the reason. No plan,
   no trade. (The trade-plan template is this.)
2. **Risk per trade small and constant** (see risk-sizing §1a); total heat capped.
3. **Measured edge**: journal every trade; compute expectancy per setup; kill setups
   that don't pay after ~30 trades. Grade decisions, not outcomes.
4. **Few setups, mastered**: 2–3 setups traded consistently beat a dozen traded
   occasionally.
5. **Trade with the regime**: size down or stand aside when the tape disagrees.
6. **Cut losses mechanically, let winners run by rule** (partial at 2R, trail the rest).
   Track avg win / avg loss. If it's below ~1.5 with a <50% win rate, the exits are broken.
7. **Pre-mortem**: before entry, write how this trade most likely fails. That is the
   counterargument field.
8. **Daily and weekly loss limits**: a daily loss limit (e.g. 2–3R) and weekly review
   stop tilt-driven revenge trading.
9. **Separate buckets**: long-term core vs. tactical swing capital; tactical losses
   never get "rescued" by moving them into the core.
10. **Checklists over conviction**: conviction doesn't lower volatility. High-conviction
    growth names can still drop 30% in a quarter. Conviction belongs in *what* to trade;
    the checklist decides *how much*.
11. **Beware deadline-driven risk**: a fixed date plus leverage plus a big return target
    pushes toward larger size exactly when discipline matters most. When the required
    return is far above what a measured edge supports, the right move is to revisit the
    target or timeline, not the risk limits.

## 4. Pre-trade checklist (10 questions, answer all)
1. Is the market regime supportive for this direction?
2. Does the stock pass the trend/RS filter (or is this a deliberate counter-trend trade)?
3. Is there a defined setup with a trigger, not just a price?
4. Where exactly is the thesis wrong (stop), and is it at structure?
5. Is the size derived from the stop and within risk %, heat, C2, C3?
6. What is the correlation to what I already own?
7. Any earnings/ex-div/macro event in the holding window? What's the plan for it?
8. Reward:risk ≥ 2:1 to a realistic target?
9. Wash-sale or short-term tax issue?
10. If this loses, will I follow the plan without adding or moving the stop?

## 5. Weekly review ritual
Open trades vs plan, closed trades graded (R, plan followed, mistake tag), expectancy
by setup, heat and margin usage, hedge P&L vs budget, regime reassessment, one
process change for next week (only one).
